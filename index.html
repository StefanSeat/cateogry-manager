Lessons from Croatia for the Serbian launch

1. Expect years of losses

Croatia is still loss-making after five years (about €40 m over four years). Serbia will need the same patience: grow traffic and sales per store first, margin later.

2. Store density matters

Croatian sales per store (about €3.6 m) are half of Italy's. A few scattered stores do not build habits or logistics scale. Open in clusters (Belgrade, Novi Sad, Niš) next to competitors.

3. Localise fresh from day one

In Croatia dry grocery comes from the Italian range, while butcher, deli, bakery and produce use local suppliers, marked "Hrvatski proizvod". Serbia needs the same: local meat, dairy (kajmak, pavlaka), bread, fruit and veg.

4. Imported prices run higher

Italian private label costs about 17% more in Croatia than in Italy. Serbia adds customs and a longer supply chain on top, so the price gap must be planned, not discovered.

5. Lidl is already there

Lidl has run Serbian stores since 2018 and is the benchmark for price image. Eurospin's answer is Italian quality private label plus sharp prices on everyday staples.

6. Build the supplier base early

Local suppliers need time to meet discount specs, volumes and quality audits. Supplier development has to start well before the first store opens.

Line to use in the interview: "Italy shows what the model can do at scale: 80% private label and a profitable number one position. Croatia shows what the first years look like: fast growth, losses while the network builds, and a range that mixes the Italian core with local fresh. For Serbia I would use both: the Italian private label as the core, a strong local fresh offer, and sharp prices on the items Serbian shoppers compare against Lidl and Maxi."

Why prices differ between markets

  1. VAT. Italy charges 4% on many basic foods (pasta, flour, bread, milk) and 10% on most other food. Croatia uses 5% on a list of basics, but 13% or 25% on a lot of processed food. Part of the gap is tax, before any margin. Check the exact rates per product before quoting them.
  2. Logistics and scale. Land, Dexal, Tre Mulini and Jerez are mostly made in Italy. Transport to Croatia and much lower volume per SKU (about 42 stores vs. 1,300+) add cost per unit.
  3. Competition and price index. In Italy Eurospin fights MD, Lidl and Conad's private label on Italian staples, so prices are sharp. In Croatia it is measured against Konzum, Lidl, Kaufland and Spar, and there is room for a slightly higher margin on imported Italian products.
  4. Business stage. Croatia is still loss-making while it builds the network, so it cannot price everything like a mature market. The question is where to invest in price.
Your point of view for Serbia: "I would pick 10 to 15 key value items that Serbian shoppers compare every week (bread, milk, oil, sugar, flour, pasta, coffee) and price them at or below Lidl from day one, funded by margin on Italian specialties and non-food. A new chain gets its price image in the first months. I would also build a local fresh range with Serbian suppliers, because local origin builds trust where Maxi and Lidl are strongest."

Category management process

The classic 8 steps. Good structure for any "how would you manage category X" question.

  1. Define the category. What need does it cover, from the shopper's point of view? (Example: "breakfast" vs. just "cereals")
  2. Give it a role. Destination, routine, seasonal or convenience. In a discounter most core food is "routine", and price-sensitive items are traffic builders.
  3. Assess it. Sales, margin, market share, trends, competitor ranges and prices, shopper data.
  4. Set a scorecard. Concrete targets: sales growth, gross margin %, availability, waste %, stock days.
  5. Pick a strategy. Traffic, transactions (basket size), profit, cash, image, excitement.
  6. Choose tactics. Assortment, pricing, promotion, shelf layout, supply chain.
  7. Implement. Listings, supplier agreements, planograms, store communication.
  8. Review. Measure against the scorecard, adjust, repeat.

Quick practice: a product costs 1.60 and sells for 2.00 net of VAT. Margin = 0.40 / 2.00 = 20%. Markup = 0.40 / 1.60 = 25%. Supplier asks for +8% on cost: new cost 1.728. To keep 20% margin the price goes to 2.16. If you hold 2.00 the margin drops to 13.6%.

Likely questions

Tap a question for an answer outline. Use STAR for experience questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result (with a number).

Why Eurospin?Motivation

Combine three things: the discount model (efficiency, every SKU counts, you see your impact fast), private label (you shape the product, not just buy it), and growth (the chain keeps opening stores outside Italy).

Add something personal: you shop there, you noticed a specific product or price you liked.

What does a category manager do, in your words?Basics

Runs a category like a small business: decides the range, buys it at the best conditions, sets the price and promo plan, and is responsible for sales, margin and stock. At a discounter, with a short range and a lot of private label, it is closer to "product owner and buyer" than at a classic retailer.

A supplier wants a 10% price increase. What do you do?Negotiation

1) Ask for the cost breakdown and check it against raw material indexes (for example milk, wheat, energy). 2) Know your alternatives: other producers, tender, different spec or pack size. 3) Separate the justified part from the rest. 4) Trade, don't give: if you accept part of it, get something back (longer validity, promo support, payment terms, volume commitment). 5) Calculate the impact on retail price and on the price index versus Lidl before you agree.

Sales in a category fell 12%. How do you analyse it?Analysis

Split the drop: is it the market, or us? Then volume vs. price, then which SKUs and which stores. Check availability (out of stocks), competitor promos and price changes, delistings, seasonality and weather, shelf space changes. Then act on the biggest driver first and set a date to measure again.

How would you decide which SKU to delist?Assortment

Rank by sales, margin contribution and rotation. Then check whether it is unique: if customers would switch to another item in the range (high transferability), delist it. If it is the only answer to a need, or brings people into the store, keep it even with low numbers. Watch out for items that support a basket (for example a specific spice for a popular dish).

Private label vs. brands: how do you balance them?Strategy

At Eurospin private label leads. Brands earn a place when they are a must-have the shopper would walk out for (a few iconic items), or to show price credibility next to the private label. The private label should match the brand leader on quality in blind tests and beat it clearly on price.

How do you develop a new private label product?Private label

Gap in the range or a growing trend, then benchmark the brand leader (spec, price, pack). Write the spec, run a tender with 3 to 5 producers, test samples (blind tasting, lab), agree price and terms, design the pack, launch, then track sales, repeat purchase and complaints for the first 12 weeks.

How do you know if a promotion worked?Promo

Compare to the baseline: incremental units, incremental margin (not just sales), effect on the rest of the category (did it just steal from other SKUs?), sales after the promo (dip from stockpiling), and traffic. A promo that adds sales but loses margin and steals from your own range is not a win.

Tell me about a difficult negotiation.STAR

Use the Maxi price-indexing story or the open-book costing approach from STAR answers. Show that you prepared (data, alternatives), stayed calm, and found a deal where both sides got something.

Tell me about a decision you made with data.STAR

Use the Glovo slow-mover audit: 62 SKUs flagged, waste down from 2.8% to 1.7%, in-stock above 92.5%. Say which data, what you concluded, what you did, and the result.

What would you change in a Eurospin store you visited?Store visit

Use your store visit notes. Be constructive: one thing they do well, one or two concrete ideas (for example a gap in the range, a price that is above Lidl, a shelf that was empty). Show you think like the customer and like the P&L owner at the same time.

Where do you see yourself in 3 to 5 years?Motivation

Owning a bigger or more strategic category (for example fresh), with proven results in margin and private label growth, and possibly leading a small team. Keep it tied to Eurospin.

High-probability questions and STAR answers

Built around the job description: product specs and sensory testing, assortment and waste, supplier negotiation, and working with Quality and Marketing.

A. Product development and quality specs

The JD asks for defining product specifications and qualitative assessments (sensory, benchmarking).

"How do you approach developing a new private label product or reformulating an existing spec?"

  1. Market benchmark. Identify the market leader or A-brand benchmark (for example standardising a 3.2% UHT milk against the national market reference).
  2. Product teardown and spec definition. With Quality, define exact physical and chemical parameters: fat, protein, acidity, shelf-life stability, primary packaging barrier (Tetra Pak vs. HDPE bottle) and pallet configuration for logistics efficiency.
  3. Blind sensory assessment. Panel tests against the benchmark (taste, texture, aroma, appearance). Eurospin PL must score equal to or higher than the benchmark at a clearly lower retail price.
  4. Packaging and compliance. With Marketing and Quality: clear labelling, allergen tracking, and shelf-ready packaging (SRP) that cuts store replenishment labour.
B. Assortment rationalisation and waste

The JD stresses category analysis, assortment management and profitability.

"How do you handle underperforming products in a high-turnover category?"

Use your Glovo numbers.

Situation. I managed an assortment where dead stock locked up working capital and pulled category margin down.
Action. I ran systematic slow-mover audits and flagged 62 zero and slow-moving SKUs for targeted 30% delisting promotions, clearing stock without brand damage. Zero-movers dropped from 10.32% to 9.66%.
Result. Food waste fell from 2.8% to 1.7%, while in-stock rate stayed above 92.5%.
Eurospin link. In hard discount a dead SKU is unacceptable because shelf space is scarce. Every SKU must earn its linear metre.
C. Supplier selection and negotiation

The JD highlights supplier evaluation, commercial terms and price competitiveness.

"How do you negotiate with suppliers when you buy private label rather than brands?"

Open-book costing. I don't negotiate list prices. I break unit economics into raw materials (for example raw milk price indexes), processing, packaging and logistics.
Volume leverage. Trade scale and simplicity for cost.
"Eurospin offers high, predictable volumes, fast pallet-in/pallet-out logistics and zero marketing spend for the co-packer. In exchange, we expect baseline production costs."
Price indexing example. I price-matched 236 top products against market leaders (like Maxi). I found 96 products priced unnecessarily low versus willingness to pay, raised them, and lifted category margin from 30.85% to 31.22% with no drop in unit velocity.
D. Commercial vs. Quality conflict

The JD specifies close work with Quality and Marketing.

"A supplier offers 6% cost reduction on a high-volume product, but it needs an ingredient swap that slightly changes the taste or cuts shelf life by 5 days. What do you do?"

Protect trust. Reject a short-term margin gain if it damages the brand. In hard discount, loyalty rests entirely on private label quality.
Check the real cost. With Quality, assess whether shorter shelf life raises store shrinkage. Spoilage can easily wipe out a 6% purchase discount.
Test it. Run a controlled blind sensory panel. If shoppers notice a drop in quality, decline, or push the supplier to find savings in packaging or logistics instead of the recipe.
